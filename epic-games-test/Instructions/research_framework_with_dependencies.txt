================================================================================
SURVEY RESEARCH FRAMEWORK WITH DEPENDENCY IDENTIFICATION
================================================================================

This framework guides you through each stage of survey research, identifying
what dependencies to uncover and what questions to ask at each step.

================================================================================
FRAMEWORK OVERVIEW
================================================================================

STAGE 1: REQUEST (Day 1)
    └── Dependencies: Sample Size, Source

STAGE 2: DESIGN (Day 2-3)
    └── Dependencies: Targeting, Data Join, Representativeness, Data Cleaning

STAGE 3: BUILD (Day 3-4)
    └── Dependencies: Validate all technical dependencies

STAGE 4: DISTRIBUTE (Day 5)
    └── Dependencies: Execute targeting, confirm source access

STAGE 5: COLLECT (Day 5-12)
    └── Dependencies: Monitor sample size, representativeness

STAGE 6: CLEAN (Day 13)
    └── Dependencies: Execute data cleaning rules

STAGE 7: ANALYZE (Day 14-15)
    └── Dependencies: Execute data joins

STAGE 8: DELIVER (Day 16-17)
    └── Dependencies: None (execution of insights)


================================================================================
STAGE 1: REQUEST
================================================================================

PURPOSE: Understand the business need and define research scope

ACTIVITIES:
□ Intake meeting with stakeholder
□ Define research questions
□ Establish timeline
□ Create research brief

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
UNDERSTANDING REQUIREMENTS: INTAKE QUESTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

These questions ensure you understand the business context before designing
the survey. Ask these in your intake meeting with the stakeholder.

............................................................................
QUESTION 1: "What decision will this research inform?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Research without a clear decision is just data collection. This question
ensures the survey produces actionable insights, not just interesting numbers.
It also helps you prioritize what questions to include.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Good Answer:
    "We need to decide whether to revert the shotgun nerf, adjust it, or 
    keep it as is. The decision needs to be made by Friday for the hotfix."
    
    → This is actionable. You know exactly what the output needs to inform.
    
    Vague Answer:
    "We just want to know how players feel about the update."
    
    → Follow up: "If the results show players are unhappy, what would you do 
    differently than if they're happy?" Force them to articulate the decision.

............................................................................
QUESTION 2: "Who will use these results?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Different audiences need different deliverables. An executive needs a 1-page
summary. A product manager needs segment breakdowns. A designer needs 
specific feedback on UI elements. This shapes your output format.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "The Game Director will review at the leadership meeting."
    → Prepare: Executive summary, 3-5 key findings, clear recommendation
    
    Answer: "The weapon balance team will use it for tuning."
    → Prepare: Detailed segment breakdowns, open-text themes, specific metrics
    
    Answer: "Marketing wants to understand player sentiment for messaging."
    → Prepare: Quotable findings, segment profiles, positive themes for campaigns

............................................................................
QUESTION 3: "What is the timeline? When do you need results?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Timeline determines methodology. A 2-day turnaround means a short pulse survey.
A 3-week timeline allows for MaxDiff/Conjoint and deeper analysis. This also
helps you push back if expectations are unrealistic.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "We need directional data by Monday (4 days)."
    → Approach: Short survey (5-7 questions), in-game distribution, quick analysis
    
    Answer: "The planning meeting is in 3 weeks."
    → Approach: Full methodology, email distribution, thorough analysis
    
    Answer: "As soon as possible."
    → Follow up: "What's the actual deadline driving this? What happens if 
    results come in a week vs two weeks?" Get a real date.

............................................................................
QUESTION 4: "What do you already know or believe about this topic?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Stakeholders often have hypotheses. Understanding them helps you design
questions that either validate or challenge those beliefs. It also prevents
you from reporting findings they already know.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "We think competitive players hate the nerf, but casuals don't care."
    → Design: Include segment analysis (casual vs competitive) to test this
    
    Answer: "Social media says everyone hates it, but we're not sure if 
    that's representative."
    → Design: Focus on representativeness; include question to identify 
    social media users vs non-users
    
    Answer: "We have no idea — that's why we need research."
    → Design: Broader exploratory approach; include open-ended questions

............................................................................
QUESTION 5: "What segments or sub-groups need separate analysis?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
This directly impacts sample size requirements. If you need to analyze
casual vs competitive players separately, you need enough responses in each
group. Identify segments upfront to plan properly.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "We need to see casual, core, and hardcore players separately."
    → Sample planning: Need n≥300-500 per segment = target 1,500+ total
    
    Answer: "We need to compare across NA, EU, and Asia regions."
    → Sample planning: Need n≥300 per region = target 1,000+ total
    
    Answer: "Just overall is fine."
    → Sample planning: n≥1,000 total is sufficient

CRITICAL FOLLOW-UP: SEGMENT CLARIFICATION (Always Document)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Rule: Every segment mentioned MUST have a clear definition documented.
Even if you think you know what it means, write it down. This prevents
misalignment later and ensures targeting/analysis uses the right criteria.

For each segment, document:

    SEGMENT CLARIFICATION TEMPLATE
    ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
    
    Segment Name: [e.g., "Casual Player"]
    
    Definition (general):
    [What does this mean in common understanding?]
    
    Definition (for this research):
    [What specific criteria define this segment for THIS project?]
    
    Data source:
    [Where does this classification come from? Self-reported? Behavioral?]
    
    Criteria/Thresholds:
    [Exact rules: hours played, win rate, ranked participation, etc.]
    
    ═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

CONTEXT-SPECIFIC SEGMENTS (For Zero Build specifically):

    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │ SEGMENT: Zero Build Main                                                │
    ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
    │ Definition: Player who primarily plays Zero Build mode.                 │
    │                                                                         │
    │ Criteria:                                                               │
    │ - ZB hours > 70% of total Fortnite hours (last 30 days), OR             │
    │ - Build mode hours = 0                                                  │
    │                                                                         │
    │ Data source: telemetry_summary table                                    │
    │ - zb_total_hours / (zb_total_hours + build_total_hours) > 0.7           │
    └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
    
    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │ SEGMENT: Plays Both Modes                                               │
    ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
    │ Definition: Player who plays both Zero Build and Build modes.           │
    │                                                                         │
    │ Criteria:                                                               │
    │ - ZB hours > 0 AND Build hours > 0 (last 30 days)                       │
    │ - Neither mode is >70% of total hours                                   │
    │                                                                         │
    │ Data source: telemetry_summary table                                    │
    │ - zb_total_hours > 0 AND build_total_hours > 0                          │
    │ - zb_total_hours / total_hours BETWEEN 0.3 AND 0.7                      │
    └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

WHY THIS MATTERS:

    Without clarification:
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    PM says: "Casual players"
    You assume: Players who play <5 hours/week
    Analytics team defines as: Players who never played ranked
    Targeting uses: Self-reported casual in survey
    
    Result: Three different definitions → inconsistent analysis → confusion
    
    With clarification:
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    PM says: "Casual players"
    You ask: "How does Analytics define casual in the segmentation table?"
    Document: player_type = 'casual' means <10 hrs/week OR no ranked
    Targeting uses: Same definition
    Survey validation: Q3 asks playstyle to cross-check
    
    Result: Everyone aligned → consistent analysis → trusted insights

QUESTIONS TO ASK FOR EACH SEGMENT:

    1. "Is there an existing definition for this segment in our data?"
    2. "Should we use behavioral data (what they do) or self-reported (what 
       they say they are)?"
    3. "What's the exact threshold that separates this segment from others?"
    4. "Does this definition change for the specific context of this research?"
       (e.g., "casual" overall vs "casual Zero Build player" might differ)

QUESTION 6: "What have you already tried or learned about this?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Avoid duplicating past research. Understand what's already known so you can
build on it rather than repeat it. Also identify if past research created
expectations you need to address.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "We ran a survey 6 months ago on weapon balance. I can share results."
    → Action: Review past survey; build on methodology; compare results
    
    Answer: "We've been monitoring social media and forums."
    → Action: Use social themes to inform question design; validate if 
    social sentiment matches broader player base
    
    Answer: "This is the first time we're researching this."
    → Action: More exploratory approach; include open-ended discovery questions

RULE: NOTE THE SOURCE, BUT REVIEW IT AFTER YOUR ANALYSIS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

To avoid confirmation bias, follow this protocol:

    1. ASK where past research/data exists
    2. DOCUMENT where to find it (file path, report name, who has it)
    3. DO NOT review the details until AFTER you complete your own analysis
    4. COMPARE your findings to past research only at the end

Why this matters:
- If you see "sentiment was 4.1 last time," you might unconsciously interpret 
  ambiguous data to match that expectation
- If you know "social media is negative," you might focus on negative signals
- Fresh analysis without prior anchoring produces more objective findings

    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │ PAST RESEARCH LOG (Document now, review later)                          │
    ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
    │                                                                         │
    │ Source 1: _____________________________________________                  │
    │ Location: _____________________________________________                  │
    │ Contact:  _____________________________________________                  │
    │ Review after: Stage 7 (Analysis complete)                               │
    │                                                                         │
    │ Source 2: _____________________________________________                  │
    │ Location: _____________________________________________                  │
    │ Contact:  _____________________________________________                  │
    │ Review after: Stage 7 (Analysis complete)                               │
    │                                                                         │
    └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

EXCEPTION: When past research IS needed upfront:
- Question wording from past surveys (if you need to track trends exactly)
- Methodology details (to ensure comparability)
- Population benchmarks (needed for representativeness planning)

In these cases, review only the specific methodological elements, not the 
findings/results.

QUESTION 7: "What would be a surprising result?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
This reveals stakeholder assumptions and helps you understand what findings
would actually change their thinking. If nothing would surprise them, the
research may not be valuable.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "If casual players also hated the nerf — we assumed they'd be fine."
    → Design: Ensure clean segment definition; probe casual players specifically
    
    Answer: "If the weapon ranked lower priority than matchmaking complaints."
    → Design: Include MaxDiff with weapon balance vs other issues
    
    Answer: "Honestly, I don't know what would surprise me."
    → Probe: "If results showed 80% positive sentiment, would that change 
    your plans? What about 80% negative?" Understand the decision threshold

RULE: DOCUMENT THE ANSWER, BUT SEAL IT UNTIL ANALYSIS IS COMPLETE
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Knowing what would "surprise" stakeholders can bias your analysis:
- You might unconsciously look for evidence that confirms their expectations
- You might over-interpret data that contradicts their beliefs
- You might anchor on their assumptions rather than letting data speak

Protocol:
    1. ASK the question (valuable for survey design)
    2. DOCUMENT their answer in a sealed note
    3. USE for survey design only (ensure you're asking the right questions)
    4. DO NOT review during analysis
    5. COMPARE to your findings only AFTER analysis is complete

    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │ STAKEHOLDER ASSUMPTIONS LOG (Seal until Stage 8)                        │
    ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
    │                                                                         │
    │ What stakeholder believes/expects:                                      │
    │ __________________________________________________________________      │
    │ __________________________________________________________________      │
    │                                                                         │
    │ What would surprise them:                                               │
    │ __________________________________________________________________      │
    │ __________________________________________________________________      │
    │                                                                         │
    │ ⚠️ DO NOT REVIEW UNTIL ANALYSIS COMPLETE                                │
    │ Review date: After Stage 7, before finalizing deliverable               │
    │                                                                         │
    └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Why still ask this question:
- Ensures your survey CAN detect surprising results (right questions asked)
- Validates that research has potential to change thinking
- After analysis: Helps frame findings ("This confirms/challenges assumptions")

What to use during survey design (OK to use):
- Topics to probe (if casual sentiment would be surprising, include that segment)
- Questions to include (if priority ranking matters, add MaxDiff)

What NOT to use during analysis (seal it):
- Their expected direction ("they think casual is positive")
- Their hypotheses ("they believe competitive wants ranked")
- What would confirm vs. challenge their beliefs

QUESTION 8: "What actions are you considering, and what would tip the decision?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Understanding the decision options helps you design questions that
differentiate between them. Knowing the tipping point helps you focus on
metrics that matter.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "We're considering: (A) full revert, (B) partial adjustment, 
    (C) keep as is. If >50% are negative, we'll probably revert."
    → Design: Measure exact sentiment distribution; understand what 
    "partial adjustment" means to probe for specific preferences
    
    Answer: "If players hate it, we'll change it. If they're fine, we'll 
    keep it."
    → Follow up: "What percentage of negative sentiment would trigger a 
    change? 30%? 50%? 70%?" Get specific thresholds

............................................................................
QUESTION 9: "Are there any constraints I should know about?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Uncover limitations early: budget for incentives, restrictions on contacting
players, technical limitations on distribution, political sensitivities
about certain questions.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "We can't offer incentives for this survey — budget isn't approved."
    → Impact: Expect lower response rate; plan for larger send volume
    
    Answer: "Don't ask about the Battle Pass — that's being researched separately."
    → Impact: Exclude those questions; coordinate with other research
    
    Answer: "We can only use in-game distribution, not email."
    → Impact: Shorter survey needed; different response rate expectations

............................................................................
QUESTION 10: "Who else should I talk to about this?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Identify other stakeholders who have input or will use the results. Avoid
designing a survey that misses key perspectives. Build alignment early.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "Talk to the weapon balance lead — they have specific questions."
    → Action: Schedule follow-up meeting; incorporate their questions
    
    Answer: "Marketing might want to add questions about messaging."
    → Action: Coordinate; balance research scope to avoid survey bloat
    
    Answer: "Just me — I own this decision."
    → Action: Proceed; confirm they have authority to act on results

QUESTION 11: "Is this a one-time snapshot or ongoing monitoring?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
This question determines everything about how you build the deliverable:
- One-time (static): Manual process, polished output, archived after delivery
- Ongoing (live): Automated pipeline, self-service dashboard, maintenance plan

Getting this wrong means either over-engineering a simple request or 
under-building something that needs to scale.

DELIVERABLE TYPE MATRIX:

    ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │                    ONE-TIME (STATIC)         ONGOING (LIVE)             │
    ├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
    │ Purpose           Answer specific question    Monitor health over time  │
    │ Data refresh      Never (snapshot)            Daily/weekly/per patch    │
    │ Format            PDF, PPT, one-time viz      Dashboard, automated viz  │
    │ Access            Sent to stakeholders        Self-service portal       │
    │ Maintenance       None after delivery         Ongoing updates needed    │
    │ Build effort      Medium (manual OK)          High (automation needed)  │
    │ Data connection   CSV export is fine          Live DB connection needed │
    └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

FOLLOW-UP QUESTIONS BY TYPE:

If ONE-TIME / STATIC:
    - "Is this for a specific meeting or decision point?"
    - "After the decision is made, will you need this data again?"
    - "What format works best — slides, PDF report, or a simple chart?"

If ONGOING / LIVE:
    - "How often should data refresh?" (real-time, daily, weekly, per patch)
    - "Who needs ongoing access?" (determines permissions/sharing)
    - "What triggers someone to check the dashboard?" (alert thresholds?)
    - "How long should we maintain this?" (1 quarter, 1 year, indefinitely)

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "I need this for the March 22 leadership meeting, then we're done."
    → Type: ONE-TIME / STATIC
    → Build: PowerPoint deck + PDF summary
    → Data: Manual CSV export from Qualtrics is fine
    → Connection: No live connection needed
    → Maintenance: None — archive after meeting
    
    Answer: "We want to track Zero Build sentiment every patch going forward."
    → Type: ONGOING / LIVE (TRACKER)
    → Build: Tableau dashboard with filters
    → Data: Automated pipeline from Qualtrics to Databricks
    → Connection: Live connection to data warehouse
    → Maintenance: Update survey each patch, monitor data quality
    → Refresh: After each patch (bi-weekly)
    
    Answer: "The team wants to check sentiment whenever they want."
    → Type: ONGOING / LIVE (SELF-SERVICE)
    → Build: Interactive Tableau dashboard
    → Data: Live or daily refresh
    → Connection: Direct to SQL warehouse
    → Maintenance: Quarterly review, add new segments as needed
    → Access: Publish to Tableau Server with team permissions

    Answer: "Monthly report to leadership on player health metrics."
    → Type: ONGOING / STATIC (RECURRING REPORT)
    → Build: Templated report format, automated data pull
    → Data: Monthly snapshot
    → Connection: Scheduled extract
    → Maintenance: Low — template stays same, data updates
    → Delivery: Email PDF on 1st of each month

HYBRID SITUATIONS:

Sometimes stakeholders want both:
    "I need a presentation for March 22, but we also want to track this ongoing."
    
→ Approach: 
    1. Build the one-time deliverable first (meets immediate deadline)
    2. Scope the ongoing dashboard as a separate phase
    3. Reuse analysis/visualizations where possible

IMPACT ON PROJECT PLANNING:

    Aspect              One-Time                Ongoing
    ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    Timeline            Fixed end date          Initial build + maintenance
    Data pipeline       Manual acceptable       Automation required
    Tech stack          Local files OK          Need DB connections, server
    Stakeholder input   Once at start           Ongoing feedback loops
    Documentation       Project archive         Runbook for maintenance
    Handoff             Deliver and done        Train stakeholders on use

QUESTION 12: "What does success look like for this deliverable?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Aligns expectations on what "done" means. Prevents scope creep and ensures
you're building what they actually need, not what you assume they need.

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "Success is the Game Director saying 'I have enough to decide.'"
    → Focus: Clear recommendation with supporting data, not exhaustive analysis
    
    Answer: "Success is the team checking the dashboard weekly without asking me."
    → Focus: Self-service, intuitive design, documentation
    
    Answer: "Success is if this changes how we make balance decisions."
    → Focus: Actionable insights, integration into existing workflow

QUESTION 13: "How should we reach players? What channel and timing?"
............................................................................

WHY ASK:
Distribution method affects response rate, response quality, and who responds.
The wrong channel can tank your research or introduce bias.

WHEN TO EVALUATE:
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ STAGE 1 (Request):  Initial discussion — understand constraints & options   │
│ STAGE 2 (Design):   Final decision — once survey length and targeting known │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Why wait until Stage 2 to finalize:
- Survey length affects channel (long survey = email, short = in-game OK)
- Targeting criteria affect channel (behavioral trigger vs. batch send)
- Segment needs affect timing (casual players online at different times)

CHANNEL OPTIONS AND TRADE-OFFS:

    Channel          Response Rate   Quality    Best For
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    In-game pop-up   High (30-50%)   Lower      Quick pulse, 1-3 questions
    Email            Low (8-15%)     Higher     Detailed surveys, 5+ questions
    Post-match       Medium (20-30%) Medium     Immediate feedback on experience
    Triggered        Medium          Higher     Specific feature feedback
    Opt-in menu      Very Low (1-3%) Variable   Bug reports, passionate feedback

TIMING CONSIDERATIONS:

    Timing Factor           Question to Ask
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    Recency of experience   "Should they have just used the feature?"
    Time of day             "When is the target audience most active?"
    Day of week             "Weekday vs weekend players — different people?"
    Relative to action      "Immediately after, or with time to reflect?"
    Patch timing            "Before/after upcoming changes?"
    Survey fatigue          "When were they last surveyed?"

EXAMPLE ANSWERS:

    Answer: "We need quick feedback — in-game is fine."
    → Constraint: Survey must be SHORT (3-5 questions max)
    → Timing: Post-match or lobby
    → Expect: Higher response rate, more impulsive answers
    
    Answer: "Players need time to think — use email."
    → Constraint: Expect 10-15% response rate
    → Timing: 24-48 hours after session for fresh but reflective responses
    → Survey: Can be longer (10-15 questions, MaxDiff/Conjoint OK)
    
    Answer: "Only ask players who actually used the weapon."
    → Channel: Triggered survey after X uses
    → Timing: After 3+ uses (enough experience to have opinion)
    → Requires: Behavioral trigger setup
    
    Answer: "We've never done in-game surveys — email only."
    → Constraint: Technical/organizational limitation
    → Plan: Work with what's available; note limitation in findings

DEPENDENCIES THAT AFFECT CHANNEL CHOICE:

    Dependency              Impact on Channel Decision
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    Survey length           Long surveys → Email (players abandon in-game)
    Question complexity     MaxDiff/Conjoint → Email (need focus)
    Targeting specificity   Behavioral trigger → In-game or triggered email
    Response rate needs     Need high volume → In-game or incentivized
    Response quality needs  Need thoughtful answers → Email with delay
    Available infrastructure What channels can we actually use?
    Stakeholder timeline    Urgent → In-game (faster collection)

QUESTIONS TO ASK STAKEHOLDER:

    1. "What channels have we used before for surveys?"
    2. "Are there any channel restrictions or preferences?"
    3. "How quickly do we need responses?"
    4. "Do players need recent experience with the feature to answer well?"
    5. "Is there budget for incentives?" (affects response rate expectations)

DEPENDENCY: SAMPLE SIZE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHAT: Minimum responses needed for reliable analysis

QUESTIONS TO ASK:

To Stakeholder:
1. "What segments do you need to analyze separately?"
   → Determines how many sub-groups need sufficient sample
   
2. "How precise do the results need to be?"
   → Higher precision = larger sample needed
   
3. "Are there any small but important sub-populations?"
   → May need to oversample certain groups

To Yourself:
4. "What is the expected response rate for this channel?"
   → Email: 10-15%, In-game: 30-50%
   
5. "How much cleaning loss should I expect?"
   → Typically 10-20% removed for quality issues
   
6. "What's my minimum cell size for cross-tabs?"
   → Rule of thumb: n ≥ 100 per cell, absolute minimum n ≥ 30

CALCULATION:

    Target responses = (# of segments × min per segment) / (1 - cleaning loss rate)
    Send volume = Target responses / Expected response rate

EXAMPLE:
    - Need 2 segments (casual, competitive) × 500 each = 1,000
    - Add 15% buffer for cleaning: 1,000 / 0.85 = 1,176
    - With 15% response rate: 1,176 / 0.15 = 7,840 sends needed
    - Round up with buffer: Target 10,000-15,000 sends

OUTPUT: Sample size requirement documented in research brief

................................................................................
SIMPLIFIED SAMPLE SIZE CALCULATION (500-PER-SEGMENT RULE)
................................................................................

DEFAULT RULE: 500 clean responses per segment you need to analyze separately.

Why 500 works as a standard:
- Margin of error: ±4.4% at 95% confidence (very precise)
- Statistical power: >95% for medium effect sizes
- Covers most tests: t-test, ANOVA, chi-square, MaxDiff all comfortable
- Buffer for cleaning: Even with 20% loss, you have 400 remaining

PREVENTION STRATEGIES (Avoid Weighting When Possible)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

It's better to get a representative sample upfront than to fix with weighting.

STRATEGY 1: STRATIFIED SAMPLING (Send Proportionally)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

What: Send invitations in the same proportions as your population.

    POPULATION                    SEND INVITATIONS
    ─────────────────────────     ─────────────────────────
    Casual:      72%              Send to 72% casual
    Competitive: 28%              Send to 28% competitive

SQL Example:
    
    WITH segment_targets AS (
        SELECT 'casual' as segment, 0.72 as target_prop, 25000 * 0.72 as target_n
        UNION ALL
        SELECT 'competitive', 0.28, 25000 * 0.28
    ),
    sampled AS (
        SELECT p.*, 
               ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY p.player_type ORDER BY RANDOM()) as rn
        FROM player_base p
        WHERE p.email_opt_in = TRUE
    )
    SELECT s.* FROM sampled s
    JOIN segment_targets t ON s.player_type = t.segment
    WHERE s.rn <= t.target_n;

Limitation: Doesn't guarantee responses will be proportional (response rates differ)

STRATEGY 2: QUOTA SAMPLING (Stop When Full)
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

What: Set quotas for each segment, close collection when quotas are met.

    TARGET QUOTAS
    ─────────────────────────────────────
    Casual:      Target 720 responses (72%)
    Competitive: Target 280 responses (28%)
    Total:       1,000 responses

Qualtrics Setup:
    1. Survey Flow → Add Quota element
    2. Set quotas by segment
    3. Action when full: End survey with thank you message

Result: Exact proportions guaranteed
Limitation: May take longer to fill underrepresented quotas

STRATEGY 3: OVERSAMPLE LOW-RESPONSE SEGMENTS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

What: Send MORE invitations to groups with lower expected response rates.

Calculation:
    
    Sends needed per segment = Target responses / Expected response rate
    
    Example:
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    Segment        Target     Response Rate    Sends Needed
    Casual         720        12%              720 / 0.12 = 6,000
    Competitive    280        8%               280 / 0.08 = 3,500
    
    Without oversampling (population-proportional 72/28):
      Total: 9,500 sends
      Casual: 6,840 sends → 821 responses (more than needed)
      Competitive: 2,660 sends → 213 responses (NOT ENOUGH!)
    
    With oversampling:
      Casual: 6,000 sends → 720 responses ✓
      Competitive: 3,500 sends → 280 responses ✓

STRATEGY 4: TARGETED REMINDERS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

What: Send reminder emails only to underrepresented segments.

Process:
    Day 1: Send to all
    Day 3: Check segment balance
           → If competitive behind target → Reminder to competitive only
    Day 5: Check again
           → If still unbalanced → Second reminder to competitive
    Day 7: Final check, close survey

STRATEGY 5: TIMING OPTIMIZATION BY SEGMENT
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

What: Send at different times optimized for each segment's behavior.

    Segment        Best Send Time          Why
    ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
    Casual         Weekend afternoon       Play for leisure
    Competitive    Weekday evening         Grind after work/school
    Mobile         Morning commute         Check phone during travel
